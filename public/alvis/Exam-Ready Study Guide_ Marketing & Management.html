1 · Concepts of Marketing & Market

Core definitions, what gets marketed, who markets, and how the field is evolving. High-yield for "define & discuss" questions.

1What is Marketing?

Marketing = identifying & meeting human/social needs — "meeting needs profitably."
  • Social definition: delivering a higher standard of living.
  • Managerial definition: the art of selling products.
  • Marketing management = the art & science of choosing target markets and getting, keeping & growing customers through creating, delivering and communicating superior value.
Exam tip: Always contrast the Social vs Managerial definitions — a favourite 2-mark question.

2Exchange & Transactions

  • 4 ways to obtain a product: self-produce, force, beg, exchange.
  • Exchange = obtaining a desired product by offering something in return.
  • Transaction = a trade of values between ≥2 parties, reached after negotiation.
  • A transaction ≠ a transfer (transfer = one-way giving, e.g. a gift/donation).
Fig: Exchange becomes a Transaction once mutually agreeable terms are reached.

7Fundamental Concepts (Needs → Positioning → Channels)

  • Needs → Wants → Demands: Needs are basic (food, shelter). Wants = needs shaped by society/culture pointed at specific objects. Demands = wants backed by ability to pay.
  • STP: Segment the market → choose Target markets → Position the offering in buyers' minds.
  • Value = QSP triad (Quality, Service, Price). Satisfaction = perceived performance vs expectations.
  • 3 Marketing Channels: Communication (ads, media), Distribution (wholesalers, retailers), Service (banks, logistics, insurance).
  • Supply chain stretches further than marketing channels — raw materials → components → final buyers.

📝 PYQ — Mid-Sem, "Marketing Mix"10M · CO2 · BL-II,III
Q. Briefly elaborate how the marketing mix will help modern marketers, with suitable examples, and explain any two marketing mix concepts in the current marketing scenario.
Model Answer
The marketing mix (4 Ps) gives marketers a structured, controllable toolkit to design an offering that meets customer needs profitably: Product answers "what to sell," Price sets value exchange, Place ensures availability, and Promotion builds awareness/persuasion. In the modern scenario it helps marketers coordinate cross-functional decisions (R&D, finance, logistics, ads) into one coherent customer-value proposition, and adapt quickly to competitive/digital shifts.

Two concepts explained (example: a D2C skincare brand):
Product — beyond the physical item, includes branding, packaging, and after-sale service (e.g. cruelty-free certification, refill packs) that differentiate it from competitors.
Promotion — today dominated by digital/social-media marketing, influencer collaborations & performance ads instead of only traditional mass advertising — enabling precise targeting & real-time feedback (a shift from the classical Promotion mix of ads/sales-promotion/PR/personal selling).

9Consumer Need Recognition & Buying Process

The classic 5-stage consumer decision process (Engel-Kollat-Blackwell / Kotler model):

  • Need recognition: triggered by internal (hunger) or external (ad) stimuli — the gap between actual & desired state.
  • Information search: internal (memory) & external (friends, reviews, ads) sources evaluated.
  • Evaluation of alternatives: comparing brands on chosen attributes/criteria.
  • Purchase decision: influenced by others' attitudes & unexpected situational factors.
  • Post-purchase behaviour: satisfaction vs cognitive dissonance ("buyer's remorse") shapes repeat purchase & word-of-mouth.
📝 PYQ — Mid-Sem, Q310M · CO2 · BL-II
Q. Explain in detail the consumer need recognition and consumer buying process with the help of proper diagrams. Support your answer with examples.
Model Answer
See the 5-stage diagram above. Example (buying a laptop): Need recognition — old laptop crashes (internal stimulus); Information search — browsing tech-review sites, asking friends; Evaluation — comparing 3 brands on price, RAM, battery life; Purchase decision — chosen brand available on discount, positive online reviews tip the decision; Post-purchase — satisfaction with performance leads to a positive review & repeat brand loyalty, or dissatisfaction (dissonance) leads to a return/negative review. Marketers must manage every stage — not just drive the sale, but support post-purchase to build loyalty.

📝 PYQ — Mid-Sem, Q45+5M · CO2 · BL-II
Q. Explain market segmentation. Describe in detail all the types of segmentation with the help of proper examples.
Model Answer
Market segmentation = dividing a heterogeneous market into smaller, homogeneous groups of buyers with similar needs/characteristics, so a firm can design a targeted marketing mix for each. It helps identify the most profitable segments and avoid wasting resources on a "mass-market, one-size-fits-all" approach.

Types (see table above): Geographic, Demographic, Psychographic, and Behavioural segmentation, each illustrated with its own example. A firm typically combines multiple bases (e.g. demographic + behavioural) for finer targeting — this is called multi-variable segmentation.
📝 PYQ — Mid-Sem, Pricing Case3+4+3M · CO2 · BL-IV
Q. Pricing is one of the critical elements of the marketing mix that directly affects a company's revenue and competitive positioning. Select a company of your choice and: (a) Analyze the pricing objectives it might pursue in a competitive market; (b) Discuss its pricing approaches; (c) Evaluate the pricing strategies implemented to achieve its marketing goals. Justify with examples & whether pricing decisions align with marketing objectives.
Model Answer (example: a smartphone brand)
(a) Pricing objectives: could include profit maximisation, market-share growth, survival in a price war, or premium positioning/image-building — e.g. a challenger brand may prioritise market-share growth over margin in a competitive market.
(b) Pricing approaches: Cost-based (cost + markup), Competition-based (matching/undercutting rivals), Value-based (priced on perceived customer value) — most smartphone brands blend value-based pricing for flagships with competition-based pricing for budget models.
(c) Pricing strategies: Skimming (high launch price for early adopters, e.g. flagship launches), Penetration pricing (low price to gain share fast, e.g. budget segment entry), Psychological pricing (₹999 instead of ₹1000).
Justification: These are aligned with marketing objectives when, e.g., skimming supports a premium brand image (objective: brand positioning) while penetration pricing supports a volume/market-share objective — misalignment occurs if a brand chasing "premium image" also runs frequent discount sales, which can dilute the very positioning it seeks.

3 · Nature of Management

Definitions, characteristics, functions, roles, skills, and the classic Administration-vs-Management debate.

1Defining Management

  • James Lundy: "Task of planning, co-ordinating, motivating & controlling efforts of others toward a specific objective."
  • American Management Association: "The process of getting work done through people."
  • Koontz & O'Donnell: "Getting things done through and with people in formally organised groups."
Management = a continuous process of Planning, Organising, Staffing, Directing & Controlling resources (men, money, materials, machines) to achieve specified objectives.
📝 PYQ — NIT Silchar, MS-401, Mid-Sem Oct 2023, 7th Sem4M · CO1 · BL-II
Q. What are the scope and objectives of management?
Model Answer
Scope of Management (areas it covers): (1) Planning — deciding objectives & course of action; (2) Organising — arranging resources & authority relationships; (3) Staffing — right people in right jobs; (4) Directing — guiding & motivating; (5) Controlling — comparing actual vs planned performance; (6) applies to all functional areas — production, marketing, finance, HR; (7) applicable at all levels — top, middle, first-line; (8) universal — applies to profit & non-profit organisations alike.

Objectives of Management: (1) Optimum utilisation of resources (men, money, material, machines); (2) Achieving predetermined organisational goals efficiently & effectively; (3) Ensuring survival & growth of the enterprise; (4) Balancing the interests of stakeholders — owners, employees, customers, society; (5) Increasing productivity while maintaining employee satisfaction; (6) Ensuring order, discipline & coordination among various departments.

★Fayol's 14 Principles of Management Very high-yield

Henri Fayol (a French mining engineer) proposed 14 principles as general guidelines — flexible, not absolute laws.

PrincipleMeaning
1. Division of WorkSpecialisation increases efficiency & output.
2. Authority & ResponsibilityAuthority (right to command) must be matched with equal responsibility (accountability) — parity principle.
3. DisciplineObedience, respect & good conduct as per agreed rules.
4. Unity of CommandEach employee should receive orders from one superior only.
5. Unity of DirectionOne head, one plan for a group of activities with the same objective.
6. Subordination of Individual InterestOrganisational interest > individual interest.
7. RemunerationFair pay for effort — satisfactory to both employer & employee.
8. CentralizationBalance between concentrating & dispersing decision-making authority.
9. Scalar ChainClear line of authority from top to bottom (chain of command).
10. Order"A place for everything/everyone, and everything/everyone in its place."
11. EquityKindliness + justice in dealing with employees — no favouritism.
12. Stability of TenureLow employee turnover — time to adjust & perform well.
13. InitiativeEmployees should be encouraged to originate & execute new ideas.
14. Esprit de Corps"Union is strength" — promote team spirit & harmony.
📝 PYQ — Mid-Sem Oct 2023, Q2(a)3M · CO1 · BL-I
Q. Production Management (PM) of an automobile company asked the supervisor to achieve 200 bicycle units/day, but PM did not give the supervisor authority over requisitioning tools/materials from Central Stores. Can PM blame the supervisor for not achieving the target? Explain the principle involved.
Model Answer
No — PM cannot fairly blame the supervisor. This violates Fayol's principle of Authority & Responsibility (parity principle): responsibility for a result should always be accompanied by commensurate authority to act. Here the supervisor was given the responsibility (achieve 200 units/day) without the authority (to requisition tools/materials). Without authority, the supervisor cannot control the inputs needed to meet the target, so accountability cannot reasonably be enforced. Fix: either delegate requisitioning authority to the supervisor, or route requests through PM promptly.
📝 PYQ — Mid-Sem Oct 2023, Q2(b)3M · CO1
Q. X Co. Ltd (PC manufacturer) directed workers to do overtime to protect market share amid rising demand & competition. Result: efficiency declined, subordinates worked more than superiors, workers became undisciplined, team spirit waned. Identify any three of Fayol's general principles being violated.
Model Answer
  1. Stability of Tenure / Overwork → excessive, unsustainable overtime harms morale & efficiency — no provision for adequate rest, effectively undermining stable, sustainable working conditions.
  2. Discipline — workers becoming "undisciplined" shows the mutual respect/obedience between management and workers has broken down.
  3. Esprit de Corps — "spirit of working together... begun to wane" is a direct violation — team harmony has been damaged by relentless overtime pressure.
  4. (Also acceptable: Equity — if overtime burden wasn't fairly distributed, or Remuneration — if overtime wasn't adequately compensated.)
📝 PYQ — Mid-Sem, "Flour Manager" case3M · CO1 · BL-III
Q. A mall's floor manager utilises all functions of management, stresses developing mental trust & cooperation among employees. Employees say they've learned a lot, meet targets, and performance/salary have increased. Identify the principle(s) of Fayol followed.
Model Answer
Two principles stand out: (1) Esprit de Corps — building "mental trust of cooperation" among employees directly reflects fostering team spirit & harmony. (2) Equity — fair, trust-based treatment that motivates employees to willingly meet targets. The rise in performance & salary also reflects effective Remuneration (fair reward for effort), making this a case of good leadership applying multiple Fayol principles together.
📝 PYQ — Mid-Sem, "Rajesh" case3M · CO1 · BL-I

📝 PYQ — Mid-Sem, "Bhatkaav Enterprises" case3M · CO2 · BL-II

There's no specialisation of jobs, no clear/fair agreements between workers & management, unfulfilled promises, strict rigid rules, and departmental (middle-level) heads openly favour their relatives for perks — causing resentment & strike threats. ★Scientific Management (F.W. Taylor)

Taylor's "Science" in Scientific Management = replacing rule-of-thumb, guesswork methods with systematic, data-based, standardised methods for doing work — determined via time-and-motion study to find the "one best way."

Key elements: Time & motion study · Standardisation of tools & methods · Scientific selection & training of workers · Differential piece-rate wage system · Cooperation between management & labour (instead of conflict) · Division of work & responsibility between managers (planning) & workers (execution).

📝 PYQ — Mid-Sem, Q1(a)5M · CO1 · BL-II
Q. What is Science in Scientific Management?
Model Answer
The "Science" in Taylor's Scientific Management refers to replacing subjective, rule-of-thumb methods of working with systematic, empirically tested, standardised procedures. It involves: (1) scientifically studying each element of a job (time & motion study) to find the most efficient method; (2) scientifically selecting & training workers rather than letting them choose their own methods; (3) providing standard tools, conditions & methods; (4) linking pay to scientifically-set output standards (differential piece rate); (5) dividing responsibility between management (planning/science) and workers (execution) so each does what they're best suited for. In short — decisions are based on data & experimentation, not tradition or intuition.

📝 PYQ — Mid-Sem, Q1(b)5M · CO1 · BL-II
Q. Identify the major difference between Classical and Behavioural school of management thought.
Model Answer
The Classical school (Taylor, Fayol, Weber) treats the worker as an "economic man" driven mainly by monetary reward, and focuses on formal structure, efficiency, standardisation and rigid hierarchy to maximise output. The Behavioural school (Mayo, Maslow, McGregor), emerging after the Hawthorne studies, treats the worker as a "social man" whose productivity is shaped by psychological & social needs — recognition, belonging, group norms, leadership style — not pay alone. In short: Classical = task-centric & mechanistic; Behavioural = people-centric & psychological. Modern management blends both.
📝 PYQ — Mid-Sem, Q210M · CO1 · BL-III
Q. In your opinion, how will technology affect and change management practices in the future, and what will remain unchanged?
Model Answer
What will change: (1) Planning — AI-driven analytics & forecasting will replace intuition-heavy planning; (2) Organising — flatter, more agile & virtual/remote structures enabled by digital collaboration tools; (3) Directing/Leading — leaders will manage hybrid & distributed teams, requiring new communication & digital-empathy skills; (4) Controlling — real-time dashboards, automated monitoring & predictive control replace periodic manual review; (5) Staffing — AI-assisted recruitment, reskilling for automation-displaced roles.

What will remain unchanged: (1) The core functions of management — Planning, Organising, Staffing, Directing, Controlling — remain conceptually the same even as tools change; (2) the need for human judgement, ethics & emotional intelligence in leadership; (3) the fundamental goal of efficient & effective use of resources to meet organisational objectives; (4) the importance of interpersonal skills — motivating, negotiating & resolving conflict remain human tasks technology cannot fully replace.

3 Management Skills

Technical, Conceptual, Human — top mgmt needs Conceptual most; first-line needs Technical most.

📝 PYQ — Mid-Sem, Q1(a–c)3+2+2M · BL I–II
Q(a). Essentials of management skills for an engineer — with justification.
Q(b). Difference between authority and power.
Q(c). Can a manager delegate his/her responsibilities? Explain.
Model Answer
(a) Skills an engineer-manager needs: Technical skill (core engineering know-how to guide execution), Conceptual skill (seeing the big picture — how a project fits organisational goals), Human skill (communicating with cross-functional teams, clients, vendors), plus Decision-making under technical & resource constraints. Justification: engineers increasingly move into project/team-lead roles where technical credibility alone isn't enough — they must plan, coordinate and motivate people, so all 3 management skills (not just technical) become essential.

(b) Authority vs Power: Authority is the formal, legitimate right vested in a position by the organisation to give orders/make decisions — it flows down the hierarchy and is positional. Power is the actual ability to influence others' behaviour — it may come from position, but also from expertise, personality, or control over resources/information, and doesn't require formal sanction. In short: authority is granted; power is exercised (and can exist without authority, e.g. an informal leader).

(c) Can responsibility be delegated? A manager can delegate authority and the task, but cannot fully delegate ultimate responsibility/accountability — the manager remains answerable to their own superior for the outcome. This is the principle of "responsibility cannot be delegated, only shared." The subordinate becomes responsible to the manager, while the manager stays responsible to the organisation.

8Planning → Organizing → Leading → Controlling (Deep Dive)

  • Planning: Strategy = Chandler's "determination of long-term goals + allocation of resources." Porter's 3 generic strategies: Differentiation, Cost Leadership, Focus. SWOT = Strengths, Weaknesses (internal) vs Opportunities, Threats (external).
  • Organizing: Departmentation (by function/product/territory/customer). Span of control = # of subordinates a manager supervises — wider span = flatter org. Centralization (authority at top) vs Decentralization (authority pushed down).
  • Leading: Motivation theories — Need theory, Equity theory, Reinforcement theory, Expectancy theory, Goal-setting theory. Team stages: Forming → Storming → Norming → Performing → Adjourning.
  • Controlling: 3 steps — measure actual performance → compare vs standard → correct deviation. Types: feed-forward, concurrent, feedback controls. Modern tools: EVA, MVA, Balanced Scorecard.

⚡ MBO in one line

Management by Objectives (Peter Drucker) = a hierarchy of measurable, mutually agreed goals across all organisational levels, built on 4 pillars: goal specificity, participative decision-making, explicit time period, performance feedback.

📝 PYQ — Mid-Sem, Q3(e–f)5+5M · CO3 · BL-I
Q(e). Define Organizational Behaviour. Explain how individuals, groups and structures work within an organization.
Q(f). Draw the basic Organizational Behaviour model. Explain diversity, personality and values as individual-level inputs in the OB model.
Model Answer
(e) Definition: OB is the systematic study of how individuals & groups act within organisations, and how that behaviour affects organisational effectiveness (see Tab-4 top definition).
Individuals bring personality, perception, attitudes & motivation that shape their performance. Groups form through shared goals/roles and develop norms, cohesion & leadership dynamics that influence how work gets done collectively. Structure is the formal framework (hierarchy, departments, reporting lines) within which individuals & groups operate — it determines authority, communication flow & coordination. All three levels interact: structure shapes group behaviour, and group dynamics shape individual behaviour, and vice-versa.

(f) Basic OB Model (Inputs → Processes → Outcomes) — see diagram below.
Diversity (individual input): differences in age, gender, culture, ability among employees — well-managed diversity boosts creativity & decision-making but requires inclusive practices to avoid conflict.
Personality (individual input): stable traits (e.g. Big Five: openness, conscientiousness, extraversion, agreeableness, neuroticism) that predict work style & fit with roles.
Values (individual input): broad beliefs about what is desirable/important (e.g. achievement, integrity) which guide attitudes, ethical choices & job-role fit (person-organisation fit).

📝 PYQ — Mid-Sem, Q610M · CO2 · BL-IV
Q. Discuss the role of Maslow's Hierarchy of Needs in understanding and enhancing employee motivation with a neat diagram. How does Maslow's theory apply in real-world organizational settings, and what implications does it have for leaders in motivating their teams?
Model Answer
Maslow's theory (see diagram/table above) proposes that humans are motivated by a hierarchy of 5 needs, and a satisfied need stops motivating — people move to the next level up. Role in employee motivation: it helps managers diagnose which need level an employee is currently focused on and design targeted interventions (e.g. someone worried about job security won't be motivated much by "growth opportunities" until security is addressed).

Real-world application: companies address physiological/safety needs via fair pay & safe workplaces; belonging via team-building & open culture; esteem via recognition programs & promotions; self-actualization via upskilling, autonomy & meaningful/challenging assignments.

Implications for leaders: (1) motivation strategy must be individualised — not everyone is at the same need level; (2) once lower needs are met, money/security incentives lose motivational power — leaders must offer growth, recognition & purpose to retain top performers; (3) neglecting a lower-level need (e.g. job insecurity during restructuring) can undo the motivational effect of higher-level perks.

9Emotional Intelligence (EI) & the Stress-Performance Curve

Emotional Intelligence = the ability to identify, understand, manage & use one's own emotions, and to recognise & influence the emotions of others — key components: self-awareness, self-regulation, motivation, empathy, social skill.

The Stress–Performance (Yerkes-Dodson) curve is an inverted-U: too little stress → boredom/under-performance; moderate ("eustress") → peak performance; too much → anxiety, burnout, declining performance.

5 · BCG Matrix (Growth-Share Matrix)

A portfolio-planning tool developed by Bruce Henderson (Boston Consulting Group, early 1970s) to classify a company's business units/products by market growth rate & relative market share.

1The Two Axes

MetricFormulaMeaning
Relative Market Share (RMS)Business Unit Sales (this yr) ÷ Leading Rival's Sales (this yr)Higher RMS = higher proportion of the market you control.
Market Growth Rate (MGR)(Sales this yr − Sales last yr) ÷ Sales last yrUsed as a measure of a market's attractiveness.
Worked example (from class): Gold price ₹30,000 → ₹40,000 over 5 years (2010–2015): Growth = (40,000−30,000)/30,000 = 33.3% total, ≈ 6.7%/year (simple annualised). India's population 3,000,000 → 5,000,000 in a decade (1990–2000): Growth = (5,000,000−3,000,000)/3,000,000 = 66.7% total, ≈ 6.7%/year.
Fig: The BCG Growth-Share Matrix — 4 quadrants.

2The Four Quadrants

❓ Question Marks / Problem Children

High growth, low share. Most businesses start here. Absorb heavy cash if share stays low. High investment needed — can become a Star, or fail into a Dog.

⭐ Stars

High growth, high share — market leaders. Need heavy investment to defend share; generate & consume large amounts of cash. Left undefended, a Star fades into a Cash Cow.

🐄 Cash Cows

Low growth, high share — "stars of yesterday" in a mature industry. Generate more cash than they need; fund the rest of the portfolio with minimal reinvestment.

🐕 Dogs

Low growth, low share — "cash traps." High cost, low quality, declining stage — limited potential; usually candidates for divestment.

3Why Use the BCG Matrix?

  • Assess the profile of a product/business & its cash demands.
  • Track the development cycle of a product.
  • Guide resource-allocation & divestment decisions.

4Main Steps

  1. Identify & divide the company into Strategic Business Units (SBUs).
  2. Assess each SBU on: (a) relative market share, (b) industry growth rate.
  3. Classify each SBU into a quadrant of the BCG matrix.
  4. Develop a strategic objective for each SBU (build, hold, harvest, or divest).

✅ Benefits

Simple & easy to understand; quickly screens opportunities; helps allocate corporate cash to maximise future growth & profitability.

⚠️ Limitations

Only 2 dimensions considered; hard to get accurate market-share/growth data; high share ≠ guaranteed profit; low-share businesses can be profitable too.

⚡ Exam One-liner

BCG Matrix = 2×2 portfolio grid (Relative Market Share × Market Growth Rate) → Stars, Cash Cows, Question Marks, Dogs — used for resource allocation across a multi-product company's SBUs. Despite limitations (only 2 dimensions, data difficulty), it remains one of the most widely used strategic planning tools.
