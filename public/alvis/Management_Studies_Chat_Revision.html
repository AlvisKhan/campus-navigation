Product Life Cycle

1. What is Product Life Cycle?

Imagine a new product entering the market. Initially very few people know about it. Gradually sales increase, reach a peak and eventually decline. Product Life Cycle (PLC) is the sequence of stages through which a product passes over time, from introduction to growth, maturity and decline. Changes in the marketing situation affect the marketing strategy and marketing mix.

2. Four stages of PLC

Introduction

Low sales, high launch and promotion costs, negative or low profit. Objective: create awareness. Example: a newly launched smartphone.

Growth

Sales rise rapidly, profits increase, competitors enter. Objective: maximise market share.

Maturity

Sales peak or stabilise, competition intensifies. Objective: maximise profit and defend market share.

Decline

Sales and profits fall. Objective: reduce expenditure, harvest or discontinue weak products.

Exam questions

What happens during maturity? Sales peak or stabilise, competition increases and differentiation becomes important.

BCG Matrix

1. What is the BCG Matrix?

Imagine a company selling four different products. Some are highly profitable, some require investment, and others are performing poorly. How does the company decide which product to invest in and which to discontinue? BCG Matrix helps make this decision.

Exam definition: The BCG Matrix is a portfolio planning model developed by Bruce Henderson of the Boston Consulting Group in the early 1970s. It classifies products or business units based on their relative market share and market growth rate.

Stars

High market share and high market growth. Generate large amounts of cash but also require heavy investment. Can become cash cows when market growth slows. Strategy: Invest and maintain market share.

Question Marks (Problem Children)

Low market share and high market growth. Require substantial investment. May become stars if successful or dogs if unsuccessful. Strategy: Invest selectively.

Cash Cows

High market share and low market growth. Generate substantial cash with relatively little investment. Their cash can finance stars and promising question marks. Strategy: Maintain and harvest profits.

Dogs

Low market share and low market growth. Have limited growth and profit potential. Strategy: Harvest, reposition or divest if unprofitable.

4. Important formulas

A. Market Share (MS) — Percentage of the total market controlled by a company.

MS = Company salesTotal market sales × 100

B. Relative Market Share (RMS) — Compares the company’s performance with its leading rival.

RMS = Company salesLeading rival’s sales

RMS > 1: High relative market share. RMS < 1: Low relative market share. RMS = 1: Equal sales.

C. Market Growth Rate (MGR) — Measures how rapidly the market is expanding.

MGR = Scurrent − SpreviousSprevious × 100

D. Compound Annual Growth Rate (CAGR) — When calculating compounded annual growth over multiple years:

CAGR = [(VfVi)1/n − 1] × 100

Vf is final value, Vi is initial value and n is the number of years. This additional formula is useful for the multi-year problems on slide 6.

Pricing Strategies

1. Price vs Pricing

PricePricing
Monetary value of a product paid by the consumer.Process of determining the monetary value of a product before offering it for sale.
Example: ₹500 for a shirt.Deciding whether to sell it at ₹500 or ₹600.

2. Objectives of pricing

Survival: Remain competitive in the market.
Profit maximisation: Earn maximum possible profit.
Market share: Capture more customers.
Market skimming: Extract higher revenue from customers willing to pay more.
Sales maximisation: Increase sales volume.

3. Four pricing approaches

ApproachPrice determined byExample
Cost-orientedProduction cost + profitCost ₹100 + profit ₹20 = ₹120
Demand-orientedCustomer demandHigher hotel prices during festivals
Competitor-orientedCompetitors’ pricesMatching a rival’s price
Value-orientedCustomer’s perceived valueLuxury branded products
Remember: Cost, Demand, Competitor, Value (CDCV).

4. Six pricing strategies (very important)

1. Premium Pricing
Charging a high price to reflect superior quality, exclusivity or brand image. Example: Rolex watches.
2. Penetration Pricing
Initially charging a low price to attract customers and capture market share. Example: Jio’s introductory telecom offers.
3. Price Skimming
Initially charging a high price and gradually reducing it over time. Example: A newly launched smartphone becoming cheaper after several months.
4. Psychological Pricing
Setting prices to influence customers’ psychological perception. Example: ₹999 appears cheaper than ₹1,000.
5. Economy Pricing
Charging low prices by minimising production and marketing costs. Example: Budget or generic grocery products.
6. Bundle Pricing
Selling multiple products together at a combined price, usually lower than their individual total. Example: Burger + fries + cold drink combo.

F.W. Taylor’s Scientific Management

1. Basic concept

Suppose a factory has 10 workers performing the same task using different methods. Some finish in 10 minutes, while others take 20 minutes. Taylor suggested scientifically studying their work, identifying the one best way to perform the task and training everyone accordingly.

Exam definition: Scientific Management is a management approach developed by Frederick Winslow Taylor that applies scientific methods to improve worker efficiency, maximise production and minimise wastage of time and resources. F.W. Taylor is known as the Father of Scientific Management.

2. Four basic principles (very important)

PrincipleExplanation
Standardisation of work methodsFind the best method scientifically instead of using guesswork.
Equal division of work and responsibilityManagement plans; workers execute.
Scientific selection and developmentSelect the right person for the right job and provide training.
Mutual collaborationEncourage cooperation between workers and management instead of conflict.
Memory trick: S–D–S–C — Standardisation → Division → Selection → Collaboration.

3. Mental Revolution

Mental revolution refers to a fundamental change in the attitudes of workers and management towards each other.

Management
Focus on increasing production rather than merely accumulating profits.
Workers
Improve efficiency and accept responsibility rather than merely demanding higher wages.
↓ Mutual cooperation → Higher productivity and mutual benefit. Exam keyword: Harmony instead of discord.

4. Important techniques and concepts

TechniqueMeaning

Time studyDetermine the standard time required to complete a task.
Motion studyEliminate unnecessary physical movements.
Standardisation of toolsUse scientifically designed tools and equipment.
Task prescriptionProvide clear instructions to workers.
Incentive schemeReward workers for producing beyond the assigned target.

Taylor’s shovel experiment

Taylor observed that workers used the same shovel for different materials. He determined an optimum shovel load of 21.5 pounds and designed different shovels for different materials. Result: Reduced fatigue, increased efficiency and higher production.

Henri Fayol’s Administrative Management

1. Basic concept

Taylor focused on how workers should work efficiently, whereas Fayol focused on how managers should manage an entire organisation efficiently. Henri Fayol is known as the Father of Administrative Management Theory.

Exam definition: Administrative Management Theory is a management approach developed by Henri Fayol that emphasises managerial functions and 14 principles for the effective administration of an organisation.

2. Fayol’s 14 principles of management

PrincipleMeaningEasy example
1. Division of WorkSpecialisation improves efficiency.Separate frontend and backend developers.
2. Authority and ResponsibilityPower to give orders must accompany responsibility.A team leader is accountable for team performance.
3. DisciplineEmployees must obey organisational rules.Reporting to work on time.
4. Unity of CommandOne employee, one boss.A developer reports to one manager.
5. Unity of DirectionOne objective, one head, one plan.A marketing team follows a common campaign plan.
6. Subordination of Individual InterestOrganisational interest comes before personal interest.Prioritising project completion over personal convenience.
7. RemunerationFair payment to employees.Providing fair salaries and incentives.
8. Centralisation and DecentralisationMaintain a proper balance of decision-making authority.CEO makes major decisions; managers handle daily decisions.
9. Scalar ChainFormal chain of authority from top to bottom.Director → Manager → Employee.
10. OrderRight person and material in the right place.Proper allocation of employees and equipment.
11. EquityTreat employees with fairness and kindness.No favouritism during promotions.
12. Stability of PersonnelMaintain job stability and reduce employee turnover.Retaining experienced developers.
13. InitiativeEncourage employees to suggest and implement ideas.Allowing developers to propose new features.
14. Esprit de CorpsPromote team spirit and unity.Encouraging teamwork rather than internal conflict.

4. Scalar Chain and Gang Plank

Advantage: Saves time and enables quick decision-making.

Organizational Behaviour (OB)

1. What is Organizational Behaviour?

Exam definition: Organizational Behaviour (OB) is the systematic study of how individuals and groups behave within an organisation and how organisational structures and processes influence their behaviour. Its primary purpose is to understand human behaviour and improve organisational effectiveness.

Example: Why do some employees perform better than others? Why do conflicts arise within teams? How can managers motivate employees? OB helps answer these questions.

9. Endowment Effect

People tend to assign greater value to things simply because they own them. Example: A person values their old phone at ₹15,000 but would only pay ₹10,000 to buy an identical used phone.
