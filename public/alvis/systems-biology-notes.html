Why it opens this lecture

Biology has traditionally been studied one molecule, one pathway, or one gene at a time — a genomics group touching the trunk, a metabolomics group touching the leg. Systems biology exists because no single "part" explains a living organism. The whole only becomes visible when the partial views are integrated.

02

What is a system?

A system is a set of things that interact with one another within an environment to form a larger pattern — one that is different from, and not predictable from, any of its individual parts. This is the core idea behind "the whole is more than the sum of its parts."

A man-made example: the Boeing 787 Dreamliner is built from roughly 2.3 million individual parts. No single bolt, wire, or panel can fly — flight is a property that emerges only once every part is interacting correctly with every other part, inside the right environment (air, fuel, control software).

Living organisms are systems in exactly this sense — except vastly more complex, self-repairing, self-replicating, and shaped by billions of years of evolution rather than a design blueprint.

03

Can life be built from scratch?

If a system can be engineered, can a living system be engineered too?

  • The Miller–Urey experiment (1950s) — simulated early-Earth conditions in a flask and showed that simple organic molecules, including amino acids, could form spontaneously from inorganic starting materials.
  • Mycoplasma laboratorium — "Synthia" (2010) — the product of a fifteen-year, roughly $30 million effort led by geneticist Craig Venter, widely reported as the first cell controlled by a chemically synthesized genome.

"We created a new cell. It's alive. But we didn't create life from scratch — we created it, as all life on this planet is, out of a living cell."

— Craig Venter, responding to CNN's question of whether he had created new life (2010)

The distinction matters: Venter's team rewrote a genome, but they still needed an existing living cell to boot that genome up. True "from scratch" synthetic life — assembled from non-living chemistry alone — remains unachieved.

04

What is life?

Life is a relationship among molecules, not a property of any one molecule.

— Linus Pauling

No single protein, strand of DNA, or metabolite is "alive" on its own. Life emerges from the relationships between these molecules — how DNA is copied, how it is read out into RNA and protein, and how those proteins act back on the DNA and on each other.

This molecular flow runs alongside several other kinds of information flow in a living system:

  • Metabolic pathwaysThe chemical flux of small molecules being built up and broken down to generate energy and building blocks.
  • Signalling pathwaysCommunication within a species and between species — cells "talking" to each other and to their environment.
  • Electrical signalsThe nervous system's rapid, wire-like transmission of information.
  • EvolutionThe flow of genetic information across generations, edited slowly by selection.

This bidirectional pyramid is why a purely reductionist approach — study the gene, then the protein, then stop — misses so much of biology. Systems biology tries to model both directions at once.

06

One Health: why it matters

The complexity pyramid does not stop at the individual organism. The One Health framework — promoted by agencies such as the USGS — recognises that human health, animal health, and environmental health are interconnected systems, not separate silos.

A pathogen that jumps from wildlife to livestock to humans, or a pollutant that moves from soil to water to the food chain, is the same "flow of information and matter" idea from the previous section, scaled up from a single body to entire ecosystems.

07

Systems biology and systems medicine

Systems biology is the study of the interaction between the components of a biological system, and how these interactions give rise to the function and behaviour of that system.

— Nir Friedman

The goal is to construct models of complex biological systems and diseases.

— Trey Ideker

Systems medicine is simply what happens when these systems biology approaches — network modelling, multi-omics integration, computational simulation — are applied directly to human disease and clinical practice.

09

The omics revolution

"Omics" technologies feed the high-throughput assay stage of the cycle above — each one profiling a different molecular layer of the system at once. Expand each card for a worked detail.

Genomics+

The complete DNA sequence — the fixed blueprint shared by (nearly) every cell in an organism, largely unchanged across its lifetime.

Transcriptomics+

Which genes are being actively read into RNA, and how much — a snapshot that changes by cell type, condition, and time.

Proteomics+

The full set of proteins actually present and doing work — the molecular machines built from the transcriptome's instructions.

Metabolomics+

The small-molecule end products of all that activity — sugars, lipids, and other metabolites that reflect the system's current state.

Worked example from the literature

Integrative multi-omics studies of schizophrenia have combined genetic, transcriptomic, and other molecular layers to move from genetic risk factors toward clinical classification and prediction — illustrating exactly why no single omics layer tells the full clinical story on its own.

10

Evolution of sequencing technologies

The omics revolution was only possible because DNA sequencing itself went through several technological generations. Tap each generation to expand it.

First generation+
Sanger sequencing — accurate but slow and low-throughput; the workhorse that produced the first reference genomes.
Second generation+
Short-read, massively parallel sequencing (e.g. Illumina) — a dramatic jump in throughput and cost efficiency, enabling population- and cohort-scale sequencing.
Third generation+
Long-read, single-molecule platforms (e.g. PacBio) — reads spanning much longer stretches of DNA, resolving repeats and structural variation that short reads miss.
Ongoing evolution+
Genome sequencing continues to move toward greater accuracy, lower cost, and portability — pushing multi-omics integration from specialised labs toward routine, everyday practice.
