⚡ The One-Sentence Case

In January 2014, three very different multinationals — CareCo (personal care), ShoeCo (footwear), and MedCo (pharma) — are each deciding whether and how to enter Ethiopia, a fast-growing but infrastructure-poor, bureaucratic, low-competition African market.

2 · Ethiopia — Historical & Economic Context

Fig: Ethiopia's political-economic timeline leading to the case.

1Political Background

  • 1974–1991: Soviet-backed military junta — human-rights abuses, corruption, famine.
  • 1991: EPRDF overthrows the government.
  • 1994: New constitution written. 1995: Formal elections — EPRDF wins, government stable & single-party since.
  • Mid-1990s onward: Private companies invited into previously state-controlled sectors; 300+ SOEs privatized; simplified business registration; revised tax code.

2Economic Snapshot (2003–2013)

Population

+30% → ~94 million

GDP

$8.6B → $47.5B

Global Ranking

#6 of 54 Sub-Saharan African countries for market opportunity

  • Growth stronger in services & agriculture than manufacturing.
  • State-led development model — public investment ≈ ⅓ of GDP (transport, power, telecom); public debt ≈ 23% of GDP.
  • New Djibouti-linked road/rail aimed to offset the landlocked disadvantage.

3Role of Government — a Double-Edged Sword

State Monopoly/Dominance

Telecom, Power, Financial services, Air transport, Shipping

Reserved for Domestic Firms

Media, Retail, Transportation, Financial services

Complaint: state-owned & party-affiliated firms allegedly had an edge in credit access, bureaucracy navigation, government tenders & customs clearance — yet the government simultaneously courted FDI in manufacturing & export sectors.

4Foreign Investment Incentives

  • Income-tax exemption up to 5 years for manufacturing, agro-processing, agricultural production.
  • Extra tax deduction for investment in underdeveloped/targeted areas.
  • Customs exemption on capital goods (plant, machinery, equipment, spares, construction material).
  • Legal protection of private property; profits/capital convertible to foreign currency — but Ethiopia faced forex shortages (imports ≫ exports).

3 · The 7 Major Investment Challenges

Mnemonic: I-H-L-F-C-I-C

1. Infrastructure
Poor cross-border trade/logistics ranking; landlocked, so surface shipments depend on Port of Djibouti. 1/3+ of population lives 5+ hrs from a city. Frequent power cuts (need backup generators); inconsistent telecom/Internet. Logistics costs expected to stay high short-term.
2. Human Resources
Cost-competitive with China for light manufacturing; 10,000+ university graduates/year = skilled, affordable labor. But gaps in work culture/management style/communication between expats & locals. Returnee Ethiopians = valuable but scarce & expensive.
3. Limited Competition (double-edged)
Underdeveloped industries → easy share capture for strong entrants — but also lets local upstarts challenge established multinationals. Low pre-existing brand awareness since Ethiopia was historically closed to foreign influence.
4. Fragmented Distribution
Centered on Mercato (Addis Ababa wholesale hub) + sub-distributors reaching rural retailers. 75%+ of population is rural. No major international retail chains — small independent kiosks dominate → high cost to serve. Importers must use domestic wholesale partners (foreign wholesaling is restricted); local manufacturers can self-distribute.
5. Cross-Cultural Adaptation
Risk of offending local norms via inappropriate marketing choices (music, spokespeople). B2B firms struggle to build trust — some used joint ventures with local firms to bridge this gap.
6. Intellectual Property
Unreliable IP protection & weak enforcement capacity. Unauthorized use of brand names/trademarks; counterfeits/piracy common.
7. Corruption & Transparency
Transparency International ranked Ethiopia 111th of 177 countries (2013) on perceived public-sector corruption. Investors cited opaque government tenders & suspected preferential treatment for some firms.
Exam tip: This list maps almost 1:1 onto a PESTEL analysis — Political (corruption, state control), Economic (forex shortage, growth), Social (culture), Technological (infra/telecom), Environmental (n/a here), Legal (IP). Use PESTEL as your structuring device if asked to "assess Ethiopia's business environment."

5 · CareCo vs ShoeCo vs MedCo — Deep Dive

1CareCo — Personal Care

Historical playbook: enter via independent local distributor (low cost/risk) → but revenue growth plateaus due to distributor's inexperience/lack of focus → CareCo has begun shifting to own subsidiaries + local manufacturing + own managers (higher capex & opex).

OptionKey Numbers
Via Distributor (import)Customs duty 30%+; gross margin 35–45%; price disadvantage vs local goods → market share could halve; upfront ~$3M + ~$3M/yr marketing
Local ManufacturingLower retail prices, bigger reach/share, gross margin up to 70%, better brand/govt control, fights counterfeits

Current addressable market: $125M (mainly urban, higher-income) — team believes true market is bigger; expected growth 15–20%/yr. Urgency driver: a competitor has already begun local manufacturing.

2ShoeCo — Footwear

China-based (1991); grew via contract/private-label manufacturing, then own emerging-market brands (2003+). Expansion playbook: 3rd-party importer when an existing offshore factory can serve the market efficiently; local manufacturing (wholly-owned or JV) when conditions are attractive — especially if the factory can also export regionally.

  • Already exports to Ethiopia via distributor — modest success.
  • Proposed factory: $40M in exports forecast; regional markets growing ~10%/yr.
  • Location: Special Economic Zone outside Addis Ababa. Capex $35M. ≥5-yr tax exemption; duty-free capital equipment.
  • ~2,000 workers, ~$50/month wage (above local average, below ShoeCo's other factories' costs).
  • Key managerial risk: finding managers who embody ShoeCo's culture and can adapt to/develop local staff.

3MedCo — Generic Pharmaceuticals

UAE-based (1983); no proprietary drugs — sells generic/private-label/localized-packaging products via lean, small-batch, agile manufacturing. Always enters new markets via wholly owned facilities to protect operational know-how. 85% of non-UAE revenue from MENA, where growth has slowed (maturity + Syria/Egypt turmoil) → pushes MedCo to look elsewhere.

  • Facility investment: $85M; operational ~12 months after ground-breaking (govt. has prioritized pharma).
  • Addressable market: $200M; expected strong long-term (15–20%) growth; healthcare spend/capita <$11 (room to grow).
  • Customers: Ministry of Health, public health system, clinics, private pharmacies.
  • Distribution: fragmented — 3,500+ mostly single-store pharmacies; ~250 wholesalers/importers (many tied to Chinese/Indian generics makers).
  • Benefits: available industrial property near Addis Ababa, duty-free capital equipment, up to 5-yr tax exemption.

6 · Exam-Style Questions & Model Answers

The classic case-study question types for this HBS brief case, with structured model answers.

Q1 · Case-Analysis Essay
Using an appropriate framework, assess Ethiopia's attractiveness as an FDI destination in 2014.
Model Answer (PESTEL structure)
Political: Stable single-party government since 1995 (positive for predictability) but state dominance in key sectors (telecom, power, finance) and perceived favouritism toward party-affiliated firms; corruption ranked 111/177 (negative).
Economic: Strong fundamentals — GDP nearly quintupled 2003–13, population +30% to 94M, ranked #6/54 in Sub-Saharan Africa opportunity — but forex shortages from a persistent trade deficit are a real operational risk (repatriating profits could be constrained).
Social: 75%+ rural population and historically closed to foreign influence mean low existing brand awareness (opportunity for early movers, risk of missteps) — cross-cultural marketing sensitivity required.
Technological: Improving but still weak — unreliable power & telecom raise operating costs (generators, redundancy needed).
Environmental: Landlocked geography is a structural cost driver — port dependency on Djibouti.
Legal: Weak IP enforcement, counterfeiting risk, restrictions on foreign wholesale/retail participation.
Conclusion: Ethiopia is attractive on growth & incentive dimensions but risky on infrastructure/institutional dimensions — attractiveness is conditional on a firm's ability to tolerate operational friction and invest for the long term (10+ year payback thinking, matching the case's Year-1-to-Year-7 projections).
Q2 · Entry-Mode Recommendation
Which market-entry mode should each of CareCo, ShoeCo, and MedCo choose, and why?
Model Answer
CareCo → Local manufacturing subsidiary (not distributor). Its historical distributor-led approach has repeatedly plateaued (per its own experience in other markets); a competitor is already manufacturing locally, so speed & control matter. The 70% vs 35–45% gross-margin gap, lower retail pricing, and better counterfeit protection justify the higher capex — recommend wholly-owned/majority-controlled local manufacturing to defend the brand's premium positioning before rivals entrench.

ShoeCo → Wholly-owned (or JV) local manufacturing factory, not just import. The regional-export angle ($40M forecast exports, ~10% regional growth) is the deciding factor — ShoeCo's model favors local manufacturing precisely when a factory can also serve neighboring markets. Tax incentives (5-yr exemption, duty-free capital equipment) and low labor costs ($50/month, still below its other factories) support a positive ROI case. Risk is primarily managerial (culture transfer), not financial.

MedCo → Wholly-owned subsidiary — consistent with its universal policy of never using local partners, in order to protect proprietary lean-manufacturing know-how. Given government prioritization of pharma (fast 12-month setup), a large ($200M) and fast-growing addressable market, and MedCo's core competency being operational efficiency itself (which a partner could dilute or leak), full ownership is the only mode consistent with its stated competitive advantage.
Q3 · Comparative Finance Question
Based on Exhibit 1, which company represents the most attractive investment opportunity, and which is the highest risk?
Model Answer
Most attractive (risk-adjusted): Arguably MedCo — largest addressable market ($200M), highest gross margins (70→75%), and a Year-7 market share target (25%) matching CareCo but on a bigger base — if the $85M capex can be justified by the market's depth and MedCo's differentiated cost/quality position. ShoeCo is the "safe" pick — lowest capex ($35M), fastest time-to-cash-flow via existing distributor relationships, and a regional-export hedge that diversifies demand beyond Ethiopia alone — but it has the smallest market and slowest growth (8–12%).

Highest risk: MedCo, by absolute dollar exposure — $85M is the largest capital commitment, in a country with weak IP enforcement (a direct threat to a generics manufacturer's pricing model if counterfeits enter) and forex shortages (risk to repatriating pharma profits). CareCo carries strategic/competitive risk — if it delays, a rival already manufacturing locally could lock up distribution & brand awareness first-mover advantage, but CareCo's capex ($55M) and market ($125M) sit in the middle of the pack, making its risk more "timing risk" than "capital risk."
Q4 · Short-Answer / Definition Style
Explain why foreign companies importing to Ethiopia are required to work through a local partner, while local manufacturers are not.
Model Answer
Ethiopian regulation bars foreign companies from wholesale and retail trade in imported goods — this protects domestic distribution businesses from foreign competition (import substitution / protectionism). A firm that manufactures locally, however, is legally free to manage its own wholesaling and distribution of its own locally-made output, since the restriction targets imported goods specifically, not domestic production. This is precisely why CareCo's shift toward local manufacturing also grants it "control of distribution, branding, and positioning" that a distributor arrangement cannot.
Q5 · SWOT (quick reference)
Summarize Ethiopia as an investment destination using SWOT.
Model Answer

Strengths

Large & growing population/GDP; government-backed infra investment; generous tax/customs incentives; low-cost skilled labor

Weaknesses

Landlocked/logistics-dependent on Djibouti; unreliable power/telecom; fragmented distribution; forex shortages

Opportunities

Low competition & brand-awareness "white space"; underpenetrated healthcare/consumer spend; regional export potential (esp. for manufacturing)

Threats

Weak IP enforcement/counterfeiting; corruption & tender opacity; state favoritism toward party-linked firms; first-mover competitors

⚡ Final Recall Card

Ethiopia = high-growth, low-competition, high-friction market. 4 entry modes trade off control vs risk (Agent → License → JV → Subsidiary). CareCo = distributor vs local-mfg margin/speed tradeoff. ShoeCo = cheapest bet, driven by regional-export cost advantage. MedCo = biggest market & capex, always wholly-owned to protect its manufacturing "secret sauce." Exhibit-1 numbers (market size, growth %, margins, capex) are your evidence bank for any recommendation question.
