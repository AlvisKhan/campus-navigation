Foundations

1.1What a graph is

DefinitionGraph

A graph G = (V, E) has a non-empty set V of vertices (points) and a set E of edges (lines), each edge joining two vertices called its end-vertices.

Self-loop

An edge whose two ends are the same vertex.

Parallel edges

Two or more edges with the same pair of end-vertices.

Simple graph

A graph with no self-loops and no parallel edges.

Degree d(v)

The number of edge-ends at v. A self-loop adds 2.

Isolated vertex

A vertex of degree 0.

Pendant vertex

A vertex of degree 1.

Adjacent and incident

Two vertices are adjacent if an edge joins them. An edge is incident on each of its end-vertices.

Reading Deo’s notation

Vertices and edges

Write G=(V,E), V={v₁,v₂,…,vₙ}, E={e₁,e₂,…,eₑ}. The book uses n for vertices, e for edges, k for components, r=n−k for rank and μ=e−n+k for nullity. An edge eⱼ is associated with an unordered pair of end vertices.

Vertex

A vertex is an object represented by a point. A crossing of drawn lines is not a vertex unless a point is marked.

Edge and end vertices

An edge is a connection between its end vertices. The same two vertices can have distinct edges, so edge labels matter in a general graph.

Finite, infinite and null graphs

A finite graph has finitely many vertices and edges; an infinite graph has infinitely many vertices or edges. A null graph has vertices but no edges. The graph with no vertices is excluded by Deo’s definition.

1.2Handshaking lemma

Theorem

In any graph, the sum of all degrees is twice the number of edges.

Σ d(v) = 2e
Proof
1Every edge has two ends (a loop has both ends at one vertex).
2So every edge adds exactly 2 to the total of the degrees.
3Hence the total is 2e. ∎
Corollary

The number of odd-degree vertices in any graph is even.

1Split Σd = Σeven + Σodd.
2The total and Σeven are both even, so Σodd is even.
3A sum of odd numbers is even only if it has an even number of terms. ∎
Worked example

Can a graph have 7 vertices each of degree 3?

1Σd = 7 × 3 = 21.
2But 21 = 2e needs 21 even. It is odd.
3No such graph exists.

1.3Special graphs

Complete graph Kn

A simple graph on n vertices in which every two vertices are adjacent. Each vertex has degree n−1, so by the handshaking lemma

e = n(n−1)2
Regular graph

A simple graph in which every vertex has the same degree r; then e = nr/2. A 3-regular graph is called cubic.

Bipartite graph

A graph whose vertices split into two sets V1, V2 so that every edge joins a vertex of V1 to one of V2. Equivalent test: it has no circuit of odd length.

Complete bipartite graph Km,n

Bipartite with parts of sizes m and n, where every vertex of one part is adjacent to every vertex of the other. Number of edges = mn.

Complete k-partite graph Kn1,…,nk

The vertices fall into k classes V1,…,Vk with |Vi| = ni. Two vertices are adjacent if and only if they lie in different classes. Number of edges:

Σi<j ninj = n² − Σni²2  (n = Σni)

1.4Degree sequences

Graphic sequence

A sequence d1,…,dn that is the degree sequence of some simple graph.

Havel–Hakimi test
1Check the sum is even and every di ≤ n−1.
2Sort in descending order.
3Delete the first number d; subtract 1 from each of the next d numbers.
4Repeat. If you reach all zeros, it is graphic. If a negative number appears or there are too few numbers, it is not.
Worked example 1

Is 3, 3, 3, 3, 2 graphic?

1Sum = 14, even. Largest 3 ≤ 4.
2Delete 3, reduce next three: 2, 2, 2, 2.
3Delete 2, reduce next two: 1, 1, 2 → sorted 2, 1, 1.
4Delete 2, reduce next two: 0, 0. All zeros. Graphic. ✓
Worked example 2

Is 4, 4, 3, 2, 1 graphic?

1Delete 4, reduce next four: 3, 2, 1, 0.
2Delete 3, reduce next three: 1, 0, −1. Negative. Not graphic. ✗

1.5Isomorphism

Isomorphic graphs

G and H are isomorphic if there is a one-to-one, onto map f : V(G) → V(H) such that uv is an edge of G exactly when f(u)f(v) is an edge of H.

Matrix form

The adjacency matrices satisfy AH = P AG PT for some permutation matrix P (rows and columns merely relabelled).

Invariants (to prove NOT isomorphic, show one differs)
  • number of vertices
  • number of edges
  • degree sequence
  • number of components
  • presence of circuits of a given length (e.g. triangles)

Both have 6 vertices, 6 edges and degree sequence 2,2,2,2,2,2, yet they are not isomorphic: one is connected, the other has two components.

Show that any sequence d1,…,dn of distinct values with n > 1 is not graphic.

Suppose it is graphic, realised by a simple graph G on n vertices.

1Each degree lies in {0, 1, …, n−1}, which has exactly n values.
2The n degrees are distinct, so every value 0, 1, …, n−1 occurs exactly once.
3Let u have degree n−1 and w have degree 0. Since n > 1, u ≠ w.

5Hence the sequence is not graphic. ∎

Paths & Circuits

2.1Walks, trails, paths, circuits

Read these with Deo’s convention: no edge repeats in a walk. A path also forbids repeated vertices; a circuit closes without repeating interior vertices. “Trail” is included as the modern synonym for Deo’s walk.

Walk

A sequence v0, e1, v1,…, ek, vk with each ei joining vi−1 and vi. In Deo’s convention, vertices may repeat but no edge repeats.

Trail

The modern term for Deo’s walk: no edge repeats, but vertices may repeat.

Path

A walk in which no vertex repeats (hence no edge either).

Circuit

A closed walk (start = end) with no repeated edge or interior vertex; a closed path.

Book convention: a,b,c,b,d is not a walk in Deo because edge bc repeats. Many modern books call it a walk; use Deo’s convention in this course.

2.2Connected and disconnected graphs

Connected graph

A graph in which every two vertices are joined by a path.

Disconnected graph and components

A graph that is not connected. It splits into maximal connected pieces called components.

Theorem

If a connected graph G is decomposed into two subgraphs g1 and g2 (every edge in exactly one), they have at least one vertex in common.

1Suppose not. Then V(g1) and V(g2) are disjoint and cover V(G).
2Every edge lies in g1 or g2, so no edge joins a vertex of g1 to a vertex of g2.
3So no path joins them; G is disconnected. Contradiction. ∎

2.3Euler graphs

Euler line (Euler circuit)

A closed walk that uses every edge exactly once. A graph having one is an Euler graph.

Euler path

An open walk that uses every edge exactly once.

Theorem

A connected graph is an Euler graph if and only if every vertex has even degree.

⇒Each time the walk passes through a vertex it uses one edge in and one out (2 edge-ends). The start vertex is left at the beginning and entered at the end. Every edge is used once, so every degree is even.
⇐Start at any vertex v and keep walking along unused edges. As all degrees are even, you can only get stuck back at v, giving a closed trail C. If C misses edges, by connectivity some vertex on C has unused edges; its remaining graph is again even, so repeat there and splice the new closed trail into C. Continue until all edges are used. ∎
Corollary

A connected graph is unicursal (has an open Euler line) iff it has exactly two odd-degree vertices; the path starts at one and ends at the other.

Fleury’s algorithm (to write the tour)
1Start at an odd vertex if there are two, else anywhere.
2At each step take an unused edge, but never a bridge (an edge whose removal disconnects the unused edges) unless it is the only choice.
3Delete the edge used and continue until none are left.

Room/door problems: make rooms (and “outside”) vertices and doors edges, count odd-degree vertices, and apply the corollary. If two are odd, the tour starts at one and ends at the other.

Complete graphs: Kn has all degrees n−1, so it is an Euler graph exactly when n is odd. Testing time: Euler graph test is polynomial (just count degrees).

2.4Hamiltonian circuits

Hamiltonian circuit

A circuit passing through every vertex exactly once. A graph with one is Hamiltonian. (Euler: every edge once. Hamilton: every vertex once.)

Dirac’s theorem

If a simple graph has n ≥ 3 vertices and every vertex has degree ≥ n/2, then it is Hamiltonian.

The condition is sufficient but not necessary: the 6-cycle C6 is Hamiltonian, yet every degree is 2 < 3. Testing time: no polynomial algorithm is known; the problem is NP-complete.

Hamiltonian circuits in Kn
1Number of distinct circuits = (n−1)!/2. Fix the start vertex, order the other n−1 vertices, and divide by 2 since reversing the direction gives the same circuit.
2Each circuit uses n of the n(n−1)/2 edges, so there are at most (n−1)/2 edge-disjoint ones.
3For n odd this is attained; for n even the count is (n−2)/2 (they leave a perfect matching unused).

2.5Round-robin tournaments

Teams are vertices and each match is an edge of Kn. In one day each team plays at most once, so a day’s matches form a set of disjoint edges (a matching). The aim is to split the edges of Kn into as few matchings as possible. The circle method gives these matchings. For even n, each even Hamiltonian circuit splits alternately into two matchings; a Hamiltonian decomposition plus the remaining matching gives n−1 rounds.

Subgraphs and operations · Deo §§2-2, 2-7

Subgraph and edge complement

A subgraph g of G uses vertices and edges of G, retaining the end vertices of each chosen edge. A spanning subgraph contains all vertices. The complement of g in G is G−g: remove the edges of g. This differs from the simple-graph complement in Kₙ.

Union

G₁={ab,bc}, G₂={bc,cd}; union keeps ab,bc,cd.

Intersection

Only the common edge bc remains (and common vertices).

Ring sum ⊕

Common edge bc cancels: G₁⊕G₂={ab,cd}.

Decomposition, deletion and fusion

A decomposition partitions edges into nonempty subgraphs; vertices may be shared. For e≥2, there are 2e−1−1 unordered decompositions into two nonempty parts.

Deleting a vertex removes its incident edges; deleting an edge retains its end vertices. Fusing two vertices identifies them and retains all edges, possibly creating loops and parallel edges.

Circuit decomposition and arbitrary traceability

A connected graph is Eulerian if and only if its edges decompose into edge-disjoint circuits (Deo Theorem 2-6). Each circuit contributes an even degree at every vertex.

An Euler graph is arbitrarily traceable from v when every choice of an unused edge eventually gives an Euler line. Deo Theorem 2-7: this holds if and only if every circuit contains v.

Hamiltonian paths and least-cost tours · Deo §§2-9, 2-10

Hamiltonian path and sufficient conditions

A Hamiltonian path visits every vertex once without closing. Ore’s condition for a circuit: in a simple graph with n≥3, d(u)+d(v)≥n for every nonadjacent pair u,v. A sufficient condition for a Hamiltonian path is d(v)≥(n−1)/2 for every vertex: add one universal vertex, apply Dirac to the resulting n+1 vertices, then remove it from the circuit.

Traveling-salesman problem

Vertices are cities; edge weights are travel costs. Find a Hamiltonian circuit of minimum total weight. For Kₙ there are (n−1)!/2 undirected circuits to compare. A minimum spanning tree has n−1 edges and need not be a tour; a tour has n edges and returns to its start.

Book application: multicolored cubes

Represent colors by vertices and each pair of opposite cube faces by an edge labeled with that cube. An orientation choice becomes a choice of opposite-face edges. In the four-cube puzzle, choose two edge-disjoint spanning selections, each using one edge per cube and giving degree 2 at each color; these describe the two pairs of opposite sides of the stack. This turns a spatial puzzle into an incidence problem.

PYQ 2024 · Q4(a)

Prove that a simple graph with n vertices and more than (n−1)(n−2)/2 edges must be connected.

Model answer

Suppose G is disconnected; we show e ≤ (n−1)(n−2)/2.

1Let one component have k vertices, the others together n−k, where 1 ≤ k ≤ n−1.
2Max edges inside each part (simple graph): e ≤ k(k−1)/2 + (n−k)(n−k−1)/2 = f(k).
3f(k) = k² − nk + n(n−1)/2 is a convex quadratic in k, so its maximum on [1, n−1] is at an endpoint.
4f(1) = f(n−1) = (n−1)(n−2)/2.
5So every disconnected simple graph has e ≤ (n−1)(n−2)/2. Contrapositive: more edges than this forces G to be connected. ∎

PYQ 2024 · Q5(a)

Prove that Kn has (n−2)/2 edge-disjoint Hamiltonian circuits when n is even and n > 3.

Model answer

Let n=2m≥4. A Hamiltonian circuit uses n edges; hence at most ⌊(n−1)/2⌋=m−1 edge-disjoint circuits can exist.

For existence, write the vertices as ∞ and the residues modulo 2m−1. For j=0,…,m−2, take the circuit

Hⱼ=(∞, j, j−1, j+1, j−2, j+2, …, j−(m−1), j+(m−1), ∞), with residues modulo 2m−1.

All 2m−1 residues occur once. The edges between residues have endpoint sums 2j−1 or 2j modulo 2m−1. These two sums for different j are disjoint. The ∞-edges use the distinct endpoints j and j+(m−1). Thus the circuits are edge-disjoint.

Therefore the maximum is m−1=(n−2)/2. The unused edges form a perfect matching. ∎

Trees & Fundamental Circuits

3.1Trees

Tree

A connected graph with no circuits.

Forest

A graph with no circuits; its components are trees. A single isolated vertex is itself a tree.

Theorem · equivalent properties

For a graph G with n vertices the following are equivalent:

  • G is a tree.
  • There is exactly one path between every pair of vertices.
  • G is connected and has n−1 edges.
  • G has no circuit and has n−1 edges.
  • G is minimally connected (removing any edge disconnects it).
  • Adding any edge between two vertices creates exactly one circuit.
Theorem

A tree with n vertices has n−1 edges.

1Induct on n. For n = 1 there are 0 edges.
2Take a tree T with n ≥ 2 vertices. Remove a pendant vertex and its edge (it exists, see below). The result is still connected and circuit-free: a tree on n−1 vertices with n−2 edges.
3Adding the vertex back adds one edge, giving n−1. ∎
Theorem

Every tree with n ≥ 2 vertices has at least two pendant vertices.

1Take a longest path v0,…,vk in the tree (k ≥ 1).
2If v0 had another neighbour, either it is off the path (giving a longer path) or on it (giving a circuit). Both are impossible, so d(v0) = 1.
3Same for vk. Two pendant vertices. ∎
Worked example

A tree has average degree a. Find n in terms of a.

1Number of edges = n−1.
2Sum of degrees = 2(n−1).
3Average degree: a = 2(n−1)/n.
4So an = 2n − 2, hence n(2 − a) = 2.
n = 22 − a
5Check: a = 1.8 gives n = 10 (9 edges, degree sum 18, 18/10 = 1.8 ✓).

3.2Distance, eccentricity, radius, diameter, centre

Distance d(u, v)

The length (number of edges) of a shortest path from u to v.

Eccentricity E(v)

The distance from v to the vertex farthest from it: E(v) = maxu d(v, u).

Radius

The smallest eccentricity in the graph. Here the radius = E(3) = 2 (highlighted below).

Diameter

The largest eccentricity, equivalently the length of the longest shortest path. Here the diameter = E(1) = E(5) = 4.

Centre

The vertex (or vertices) whose eccentricity equals the radius. Here the centre is vertex 3. Every tree has exactly one or two centres (two when the diameter is odd).

How to find the centre of a tree
1Delete all pendant vertices (and their edges).
2Repeat on the smaller tree until one vertex or one edge (two vertices) remains.
3What remains is the centre. Radius = E(centre).
Why a tree has one or two centres · Deo Theorem 3-9

For a tree on at least three vertices, deleting all pendant vertices reduces every remaining vertex’s eccentricity by exactly 1: a farthest vertex is pendant, and its neighbor becomes one step closer. The vertices of minimum eccentricity therefore stay the same. Repeat until one vertex or two adjacent vertices remain; those are the centre.

For a tree, radius = ⌈diameter/2⌉, so the diameter is either twice the radius or one less. In every connected graph, radius ≤ diameter ≤ 2·radius: choose a centre c and apply d(u,v)≤d(u,c)+d(c,v).

3.3Spanning trees

Spanning tree

A subgraph of a connected graph G that is a tree and contains all vertices of G.

Branch

An edge of the spanning tree T. There are n−1 branches.

Chord

An edge of G not in T. There are e−n+1 chords.

Rank and nullity

For a graph with n vertices, e edges and k components: rank r = n−k (branches of a spanning forest), nullity μ = e−n+k (chords). r + μ = e.

Fundamental circuit

The unique circuit created by adding one chord to the spanning tree T. There are μ of them, one per chord.

Here n = 6, e = 8, so the tree has 5 branches and 3 chords (de, ef, bf), giving 3 fundamental circuits.

3.4Minimum spanning tree

Kruskal’s algorithm
1Sort edges by weight, smallest first.
2Take the next edge unless it forms a circuit with those already taken.
3Stop when n−1 edges are taken.
Prim’s algorithm
1Start with any vertex.
2Repeatedly add the cheapest edge joining a tree vertex to a non-tree vertex.
3Stop when all vertices are included.
Reverse-delete strategy (as in PYQ 2023 Q5)

Repeatedly delete the costliest edge whose deletion does not disconnect the graph. Why it gives the minimum: an edge deleted this way is a maximum-weight edge on a circuit. There exists an MST avoiding that edge: if an MST contains it, remove it and use another edge of that circuit crossing the resulting partition, of no greater weight. This also handles tied weights. The final graph is connected and circuit-free, a spanning tree of least weight.

Rooted and binary trees · Deo §3-5

Rooted tree and levels

A rooted tree distinguishes one vertex as the root (Deo marks it with a triangle). A vertex at distance lᵢ from the root is at level lᵢ. The root has level 0; height is the greatest level.

Binary tree: the book’s definition

Exactly one vertex has degree 2 (the root); every other vertex has degree 1 or 3. Thus every internal vertex has two children. This is stricter than the modern “at most two children” convention.

If p is the number of pendant vertices, n=2p−1 and the number of internal vertices is p−1. Proof: 2(n−1)=p+2+3(n−p−1), hence n=2p−1.

Minimum height = ⌈log₂(n+1)⌉−1; maximum height = (n−1)/2. At level l there are at most 2ˡ vertices.

External and weighted path length

External path length is Σlⱼ over pendant vertices. Weighted path length is Σwⱼlⱼ, with positive pendant weights wⱼ. Frequent outcomes should be nearer the root.

For weights .50,.30,.15,.05, merge .05+.15=.20, then .20+.30=.50, then .50+.50=1. Pendant levels 1,2,3,3 give .50+2(.30)+3(.15)+3(.05)=1.70.

Counting and exchanging trees · Deo §§3-6, 3-9, 3-10

Labeled versus unlabeled trees

Cayley’s theorem: there are nn−2 labeled trees on n≥2 vertices. A labeled graph distinguishes vertex names; an unlabeled count identifies isomorphic drawings. On four vertices there are 16 labeled trees but only two unlabeled shapes.

Tree exchange, distance and enumeration

Add a chord to T to form its fundamental circuit; deleting any branch on that circuit yields a different spanning tree. Every spanning tree can be reached through such exchanges.

For trees T₁,T₂ on the same connected graph, distance = |E(T₁)∖E(T₂)| = ½|E(T₁)⊕E(T₂)|. If s branches are common, distance=n−1−s.

To enumerate: choose n−1 edges, retain exactly the connected selections, and avoid duplicates. Alternatively, use successive branch–chord exchanges. Every bridge must occur in every spanning tree. A graph’s circuits are ring sums of its fundamental circuits.

Cut-sets & Connectivity

4.1Cut-sets and cut-vertices

Cut-set

A minimal set of edges whose removal disconnects the graph. “Minimal” means no proper subset of it disconnects the graph.

Cut-vertex

A vertex whose removal (with its edges) increases the number of components. A graph with a cut-vertex is separable.

Bridge

A cut-set consisting of a single edge.

Fundamental cut-set

For a spanning tree T and branch b, deleting b splits T into two parts. The set of b together with all chords joining the two parts is the fundamental cut-set of b.

Facts to remember
  • Every cut-set shares at least one edge with every spanning tree.
  • Every circuit meets every cut-set in an even number of edges.
  • The ring sum A ⊕ B (edges in exactly one of A, B) of two cut-sets is a cut-set or an edge-disjoint union of cut-sets.

4.2Connectivity

Edge connectivity λ

The number of edges in a smallest cut-set.

Vertex connectivity κ

The minimum number of vertices whose removal disconnects the graph. Deo gives this definition for noncomplete graphs with at least three vertices. With the standard extension κ(Kₙ)=n−1, removal leaving one vertex is also allowed.

κ ≤ λ ≤ δ (δ = minimum degree)

Separability, paths and generalized isomorphism · Deo §§4-5, 4-7, 4-8

Nonintersecting paths and connectivity

Internally vertex-disjoint paths share only their terminal vertices. Edge-disjoint paths may share intermediate vertices. The minimum separator size between nonadjacent vertices equals the maximum number of internally vertex-disjoint paths; the edge version uses edge-disjoint paths.

A nonseparable graph with at least three vertices has every vertex pair on a common circuit. A cut-vertex v exists exactly when some x,y have every x–y path through v.

For fixed n,e, vertex connectivity cannot exceed ⌊2e/n⌋. Highly connected constructions achieve this bound; equal degrees alone do not prove high connectivity.

Block and 1-isomorphism

A block is a maximal nonseparable subgraph. Splitting a cut-vertex into separate copies separates its blocks. Two graphs are 1-isomorphic when repeated splitting makes them isomorphic. Splitting adds 1 to both n and k, so r=n−k and μ=e−r are unchanged.

2-isomorphism and circuit correspondence

At a separating vertex pair x,y, split the graph into g₁,g₂ with terminals x₁,y₁ and x₂,y₂. Rejoin crosswise: x₁ with y₂, y₁ with x₂. This is Deo’s operation 2 (a twist). Together with cut-vertex splitting, it defines 2-isomorphism.

Isomorphism ⇒ 1-isomorphism ⇒ 2-isomorphism; converses need not hold. For connectivity at least 3, they coincide. Rank and nullity are preserved, but equal rank and nullity alone do not imply these equivalences.

Whitney’s theorem (Deo 4-15): two graphs are 2-isomorphic if and only if an edge bijection preserves their circuits in both directions.

Cut-set and circuit ring sums

A circuit crosses a cut an even number of times: each crossing to the other side needs a return. Every cut-set meets every spanning tree.

Fundamental cut-sets form a basis for cuts under ⊕; fundamental circuits form a basis for even-degree edge subgraphs. The ring sum of two identical cut-sets is the null graph; for distinct cut-sets it is a cut-set or an edge-disjoint union of cut-sets.
