MODULE 00

What is a supply chain, really?

Strip away the jargon: a supply chain is every step, person, and system involved in turning raw materials into something a customer can use — and getting it to them.

The simplest definition

A supply chain is the network of organizations, people, activities, information, and resources involved in moving a product or service from raw material to end customer. Supply Chain Management (SCM) is the deliberate coordination of that network to deliver value at the lowest possible total cost.

A real example: your coffee cup

Beans are grown in Brazil (source) → roasted at a facility (make) → packed and trucked to a distribution center (deliver) → stocked at your local café (retail) → served to you (consumption). Packaging, labels, and even the cup itself have their own mini supply chains feeding into this one.

TIP

Interviewers love this test: if asked "explain supply chain to a 5-year-old," use a concrete product (a T-shirt, a phone, a pizza) and walk through it step by step. It shows you can translate theory into plain language — a skill they're explicitly screening for.

MODULE 01 · THE CORE FRAMEWORK

The 5 stages every supply chain runs on (SCOR model)

The Supply Chain Operations Reference (SCOR) model breaks any supply chain into five repeating stages. This is the mental model to reach for whenever you're asked to "walk through" a supply chain.

Balancing expected demand against available resources. Involves demand forecasting, inventory planning, and capacity planning so the rest of the chain isn't caught flat-footed.
  • Key question: "How much will we need, and when?"
  • Owned by: S&OP (Sales & Operations Planning) teams
Finding, qualifying, and buying the raw materials or components needed to make the product. Includes supplier selection, negotiation, purchase orders, and receiving.
  • Key question: "Who do we buy from, and on what terms?"
  • Owned by: Procurement / Purchasing teams
Converting raw materials into a finished product — manufacturing, assembly, quality testing, and packaging.
  • Key question: "How do we build it efficiently and to spec?"
  • Owned by: Operations / Manufacturing teams
Warehousing, order management, and transportation — getting the finished product to the customer, on time and intact.
  • Key question: "How does it get there, and how fast?"
  • Owned by: Logistics / Distribution teams
Handling returns, repairs, recycling, and excess inventory — often called reverse logistics. Frequently overlooked, but a huge cost and customer-experience lever (think e-commerce returns).
  • Key question: "What happens when it comes back?"
  • Owned by: Customer service + reverse logistics teams
TIP

Structure your answers around SCOR. If asked something broad like "tell me about a supply chain project you led," mentally slot your example into Plan / Source / Make / Deliver / Return — it instantly makes your answer sound organized and expert-level, even if you never say the word "SCOR" out loud.

MODULE 02

Every supply chain moves three things at once

Products don't just move one way. Three flows run in parallel — mixing them up in an interview is a common beginner mistake.

📦 Material flow

The physical movement — raw materials, components, and finished goods traveling from supplier to customer. This is the flow most people picture first.

💳 Financial flow

Money moving in the opposite direction — payments, credit terms, invoices, and rebates flowing from customer back to supplier.

📊 Information flow

The two-way exchange of data — orders, forecasts, shipment tracking, inventory levels — that lets every party coordinate. Poor information flow is the #1 cause of supply chain chaos.

MODULE 03

Inventory management essentials

Inventory is money sitting on a shelf. Every SCM interview probes how well you understand the trade-off between having enough stock and having too much.

Reading the sawtooth: reorder point in action

Stock depletes steadily → hits the reorder point → an order is placed → lead time passes → stock is replenished before it hits zero (thanks to safety stock).

Safety Stock

Buffer inventory held to protect against demand spikes or supplier delays. Too little = stockouts; too much = tied-up cash.

EOQ (Economic Order Quantity)

EOQ = √(2DS / H)

D = annual demand, S = ordering cost per order, H = holding cost per unit. It finds the order size that minimizes total ordering + holding cost.

ABC Analysis

Classifies inventory by value contribution: A items (≈20% of SKUs, ≈80% of value) get tight control; C items (bulk of SKUs, low value) get light-touch management.

JIT (Just-In-Time)

Materials arrive exactly when needed for production — minimizes holding cost but raises exposure to disruption (famously exposed during COVID-19).

JIC (Just-In-Case)

The opposite philosophy — carry buffer stock deliberately to absorb shocks. Trades cost efficiency for resilience.

Reorder Point

ROP = (Avg. daily usage × Lead time) + Safety stock

The stock level that automatically triggers a new purchase order.

TIP

Know the JIT vs. JIC trade-off cold. A very common interview question is "would you recommend JIT or JIC for [industry]?" — there's no universally right answer; the strong answer explains the trade-off (cost efficiency vs. resilience) and ties it to the specific product's demand volatility and disruption risk.

MODULE 04

Demand forecasting & the bullwhip effect

Forecasting predicts what customers will want. When it breaks down across a chain, you get the single most-asked concept in SCM interviews: the bullwhip effect.

Qualitative methods

Used when there's little historical data — expert judgment, market research, Delphi method, sales-force polling. Common for new product launches.

Quantitative methods

Uses historical data patterns — moving averages, exponential smoothing, regression, and increasingly, ML-based demand sensing.

🐂 The Bullwhip Effect

Small fluctuations in customer demand get amplified as each tier reacts and over-corrects — the further upstream, the wilder the swings.

Causes: order batching, price fluctuations/promotions, demand forecasting based on the last order (not real demand), and rationing games during shortages.

Fixes: sharing real point-of-sale data upstream, smaller/more frequent orders, stable pricing, and Vendor Managed Inventory (VMI).

TIP

This is a favorite whiteboard question. Be ready to both define the bullwhip effect AND name 2–3 causes and 2–3 fixes without prompting — interviewers often follow up "how would you reduce it?" immediately after you define it.

MODULE 05

Procurement & sourcing

Procurement is about getting the right inputs, from the right supplier, at the right cost — without creating risk.

Single vs. Multiple Sourcing

Single sourcing (one supplier) enables volume discounts and closer relationships but concentrates risk. Multiple sourcing spreads risk and increases negotiating leverage, at the cost of complexity and weaker per-supplier volume.

Make vs. Buy

Decide whether to produce a component in-house ("make," more control, higher fixed cost) or purchase it from a supplier ("buy," more flexibility, potential dependency). Driven by core-competency, cost, and capacity analysis.

Supplier evaluation criteria

Cost, quality (defect rate), delivery reliability, financial stability, capacity, and increasingly — sustainability and ethical labor practices.

Incoterms (know 3–4)

EXW — buyer handles everything from seller's dock. FOB — seller delivers to the ship; buyer takes over from there. CIF — seller pays cost, insurance, freight to destination port. DDP — seller delivers, duty paid, all the way to buyer's door.

TIP

You don't need to memorize all 11 Incoterms. Knowing EXW, FOB, CIF, and DDP — and being able to say who bears cost/risk at each handoff point — covers 90% of what gets asked.

MODULE 06

Warehousing & distribution

Warehouses aren't just storage — they're where orders get assembled, quality-checked, and staged for the final leg to the customer.

Core functions

Receiving, put-away, storage, order picking, packing, and shipping (often abbreviated as the "warehouse cycle").

Cross-docking

Goods move directly from inbound truck to outbound truck with little or no storage in between — cuts handling cost and speeds delivery. Common in grocery and retail replenishment.

DC network design

Deciding how many distribution centers to run and where to place them — a trade-off between transportation cost (fewer, bigger DCs) and delivery speed (more, smaller DCs closer to customers).

FIFO vs. LIFO

FIFO (First-In-First-Out): oldest stock ships first — essential for perishables. LIFO (Last-In-First-Out): newest stock ships first — used for non-perishables or accounting purposes in some regions.

Pick methods

Discrete picking (one order at a time), batch picking (multiple orders together), and zone picking (pickers assigned to warehouse zones) — chosen based on order volume and SKU variety.

3PL / 4PL

3PL (third-party logistics) — outsourced warehousing/transportation execution. 4PL — a step further; a partner that manages the entire logistics strategy and other 3PLs on your behalf.

MODULE 07

Transportation: the cost vs. speed trade-off

Every mode of transport trades cost against speed and flexibility. Interviewers want to see you can reason about which mode fits which scenario.

ModeSpeedCostBest for
✈️ Air
Urgent, high-value, low-volume goods
🚢 Ocean/Sea
Bulk, non-urgent, international volume
🚂 Rail
Heavy, bulky goods over land, medium distance
🚚 Road/Truck
Door-to-door flexibility, short-to-medium haul
🛢️ Pipeline

TIP

"Would you ship by air or sea?" is a classic trade-off question. Anchor your answer to total landed cost and urgency: air wins when stockout cost or time-sensitivity outweighs the freight premium (e.g. a new phone launch); sea wins for predictable, high-volume, non-urgent replenishment.

MODULE 08

Supply chain strategy

Two classic strategic axes come up constantly: how you trigger production (push vs. pull), and how you optimize the chain (lean vs. agile).

Push strategy

Production is triggered by a forecast — goods are made and pushed into the market before actual demand is confirmed. Efficient for stable, predictable demand (e.g. FMCG, groceries) but risks overstock if the forecast is wrong.

Pull strategy

Production is triggered by actual customer orders — nothing is made until demand is confirmed. Minimizes excess inventory (think Dell's build-to-order model) but can mean longer lead times for the customer.

Push-Pull (hybrid)

The chain is "push" up to a decoupling point (e.g. generic sub-assemblies built to forecast), then "pull" from there (final assembly/customization happens only after the order arrives). This is how most modern manufacturers — automotive, electronics — actually operate.

Lean supply chain

Focused on eliminating waste and minimizing cost — works best with stable, predictable demand. Think automotive manufacturing.

Agile supply chain

Focused on speed and flexibility to respond to volatile, unpredictable demand — works best in fast fashion or trend-driven electronics.

TIP

"Leagile" (lean + agile, split at a decoupling point) is a great answer when asked to design a strategy for a company with both stable core products and volatile seasonal ones — name it and you'll stand out.

MODULE 09

The KPIs every interviewer expects you to know

Numbers signal competence. Being able to define and calculate these on the spot is one of the highest-leverage things you can prepare.

Inventory Turnover

COGS ÷ Avg. Inventory

How many times inventory is sold and replaced in a period. Higher = more efficient (usually).

OTIF

On-Time AND In-Full orders ÷ Total orders

The gold-standard delivery reliability metric — both conditions must be met.

Fill Rate

Units shipped ÷ Units ordered

How much of a customer's order you can fulfill from available stock.

Perfect Order Rate

% of orders: on-time + complete + damage-free + correct docs

The strictest composite delivery metric — all four conditions must hold.

Cash-to-Cash Cycle

DIO + DSO − DPO

Days between paying suppliers and collecting cash from customers. Shorter = healthier cash flow.

Lead Time

Order placed → Order received

Total time from triggering an order to having it in hand. A core lever for customer satisfaction.

TIP

Quantify your own experience. If you have any supply chain, ops, or retail experience — even a part-time job — reframe it with a metric: "I reduced stockouts by X%" beats "I helped manage inventory" every time.

MODULE 10

Technology powering modern supply chains

You don't need to be technical, but naming the right system for the right job shows real-world fluency.

ERP

Enterprise Resource Planning — integrates finance, HR, inventory, and orders into one system (e.g. SAP, Oracle).

WMS

Warehouse Management System — runs day-to-day warehouse ops: receiving, picking, packing.

TMS

Transportation Management System — plans routes, selects carriers, and tracks freight.

RFID / Barcode

Automated identification and tracking of items as they move through the chain.

EDI

Electronic Data Interchange — standardized digital document exchange (POs, invoices) between trading partners.

IoT sensors

Real-time tracking of location, temperature, and condition — critical for cold-chain and pharma.

Blockchain

Immutable shared ledger — used for provenance/traceability (e.g. verifying ethically-sourced materials).

AI / ML

Powers demand forecasting, predictive maintenance, dynamic routing, and anomaly detection.

MODULE 11

Risk & resilience

Since 2020, "how would you make a supply chain more resilient" has become one of the most common interview questions in the field.

Common disruption types

Natural disasters, geopolitical conflict/tariffs, single-supplier failure, sudden demand shocks, cyberattacks, and labor disputes/port strikes.

Mitigation strategies

Dual/multiple sourcing, strategic safety stock, nearshoring or "friend-shoring," end-to-end visibility tools, and formal business continuity planning.

TIP

Reference a real event if you can — the 2021 Suez Canal blockage, COVID-19 chip shortages, or recent tariff changes. Connecting theory to a real headline instantly signals that you follow the industry, not just the textbook.

MODULE 12 · THE ACTUAL INTERVIEW

Question bank, by category

Use the STAR method (Situation, Task, Action, Result) for every behavioral question below — it's the format interviewers are trained to listen for.

"Tell me about a time you had to resolve a supply disruption."

"Describe a conflict you had with a supplier or vendor and how you resolved it."

"Tell me about a time you had to make a decision with incomplete data."

"Give an example of when you improved a process — what was the measurable result?"

"What is the bullwhip effect and how would you reduce it?"

"Walk me through the EOQ formula and what it's used for."

"What's the difference between JIT and JIC inventory strategies?"

"Explain the difference between a 3PL and a 4PL."

"Our lead time just doubled overnight — walk me through how you'd diagnose and fix it."

"Design a distribution network for a company expanding into 3 new regions."

"A key supplier just went bankrupt. What's your first move?"

"How would you decide between air freight and ocean freight for a new SKU?"

TIP

Always end case questions with a trade-off, not a single answer. Interviewers are testing judgment, not a memorized "correct" response — showing you weighed cost vs. speed vs. risk is more impressive than picking the "right" mode of transport.
